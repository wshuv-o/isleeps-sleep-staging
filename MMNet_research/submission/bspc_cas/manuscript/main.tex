\begin{abstract}
Automated polysomnography after ischemic stroke presents challenges arising from altered sleep-related neural activity and concurrent respiratory disturbances. Existing stroke-specific approaches have primarily addressed sleep-stage classification, leaving the joint analysis of sleep architecture and respiratory events insufficiently investigated. Furthermore, adapting pretrained EEG encoders introduces a representation inconsistency when downstream models are trained on embeddings from previously observed patients but evaluated on unseen individuals. We propose MM-Net, a multimodal multi-task framework that addresses this inconsistency through cross-fitting and ridge-based embedding alignment. Sleep-adapted EEG--EOG representations and engineered neural descriptors are integrated with learned cardiorespiratory features through cross-attention and shared temporal modeling to support simultaneous sleep staging and respiratory-event detection. On iSLEEPS, comprising 99 patients with subacute ischemic stroke, ten-fold patient-independent evaluation achieved 76.3\% sleep-staging accuracy (Cohen's $\kappa = 0.656$) and 0.825 respiratory-event AUC, outperforming the evaluated baselines under matched protocols. Controlled experiments demonstrated that embedding alignment improved staging accuracy from 75.7\% to 76.9\% over cross-fitted adaptation without alignment. The framework also achieved 86.4\% staging accuracy on healthy Sleep-EDF, the highest reported in the study's benchmark comparison, and 80.3\% staging accuracy with 0.889 respiratory AUC on ISRUC-I, exceeding its retrained staging baselines. Without retraining, the iSLEEPS-trained model retained respiratory AUCs of 0.833 on ISRUC-I and 0.841 on UCD. These findings support representation-consistent neural adaptation and joint physiological modeling across stroke, healthy-sleep, and sleep-disorder cohorts.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Sleep disturbances are common after ischemic stroke and have been associated with neurological recovery and functional outcomes~\cite{bassetti2006sleep,denis2024sleep,tellenbach2023brainstem,johnson2010frequency}. Sleep-disordered breathing is particularly prevalent in this population, making the assessment of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbance relevant to post-stroke evaluation. Polysomnography (PSG) records the physiological information required for both assessments, including electroencephalographic (EEG), electrooculographic (EOG), electromyographic (EMG), respiratory, and cardiovascular signals. Sleep-stage scoring primarily depends on neural and muscular activity, whereas respiratory-event identification relies on changes in ventilation, respiratory effort, oxygen saturation, and related physiological responses. Their concurrent analysis therefore requires a model capable of learning from heterogeneous signals whose relevance differs across prediction tasks.

Automated sleep staging has progressed from convolutional and recurrent architectures to attention-based sequence models and pretrained neural representations~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet,phan2019seqsleepnet,eldele2021attnsleep,supratak2020tinysleepnet,labram2024,cbramod2025,eegpt2024,biot2023}. Multimodal approaches have expanded this direction by integrating physiological signals, temporal context, and complementary representations. Recent studies published in Biomedical Signal Processing and Control, including MMS-SleepNet~\cite{mmssleepnet2025} and CrossFusionSleepNet~\cite{crossfusionsleepnet2026}, demonstrate the growing use of physiological knowledge, modality-specific encoding, and attention-based fusion for sleep staging. Respiratory-event detection has developed alongside these methods, and joint approaches have established the feasibility of predicting sleep stages and respiratory events within a shared framework. Huttunen \emph{et al.}~\cite{huttunen2023simultaneous} evaluated simultaneous scoring in 877 clinical PSG recordings, while subsequent multi-task models have investigated related problems in other sleep populations~\cite{multitask2025elderly}. These developments establish a methodological foundation for joint sleep analysis, although their evaluation has primarily involved general sleep-disorder, suspected-apnea, or elderly cohorts.

The application of these methods to ischemic stroke introduces additional physiological and computational considerations. Cerebrovascular injury can alter sleep spindles, slow-wave activity, and hemispheric EEG characteristics~\cite{gottselig2002stroke,fernandez2019spindles,facchin2020slowwaves,simpson2023laterality,zheng2025spindles,anastasi2017bsi}, potentially affecting representations learned from healthy or conventional sleep-disorder populations. The recently released iSLEEPS dataset~\cite{maiti2026isleeps} provides multimodal overnight PSG recordings from 100 patients with ischemic stroke, including expert annotations of sleep stages and respiratory events. Its initial analysis established sleep-staging baselines for this population. Bose \emph{et al.}~\cite{bose2026gap} subsequently investigated the generalization of EEG-based staging models between healthy and stroke cohorts, reporting performance degradation and changes in the physiological patterns emphasized by the model. More recently, Alhazmi~\cite{alhazmi2026sleepstagenet} introduced SleepStageNet, a compact five-channel staging architecture evaluated on 95 iSLEEPS recordings, together with physiological attribution analyses and transfer to an independent healthy-sleep cohort. Collectively, these studies have advanced stroke-specific sleep classification and demonstrated the importance of population-dependent evaluation. The joint prediction of sleep architecture and respiratory-event onset from stroke PSG, however, remains insufficiently investigated.

A separate methodological difficulty arises when pretrained EEG encoders are adapted to a clinical cohort and subsequently used to train a downstream prediction model. Adaptation can improve the relevance of learned representations to post-stroke sleep, but embeddings generated for patients used during encoder training may differ from those produced for previously unseen patients. Cross-fitting addresses the in-sample dependence by generating downstream training embeddings from encoders that have not observed the corresponding patients, consistent with the cross-fitting principle of double machine learning~\cite{chernozhukov2018dml}. However, independently adapted encoders may produce representations in different feature spaces, introducing an additional inconsistency with the encoder used at deployment. Consequently, reliable integration requires both out-of-sample representation generation and alignment with the deployed encoder's embedding space. The contribution of these operations can be evaluated directly through controlled encoder-integration comparisons.

To address these problems, we develop MM-Net, a multimodal multi-task framework for joint sleep-stage classification and respiratory-event-onset detection. The neural stream combines physiologically defined descriptors with embeddings from a sleep-adapted LaBraM encoder~\cite{labram2024}. Cross-fitting produces out-of-sample representations for downstream training, and ridge-based alignment maps these representations into the embedding space of the encoder used for inference. A complementary convolutional stream extracts information directly from cardiorespiratory waveforms. Cross-attention and bidirectional temporal modeling integrate the representations, while task-specific prediction pathways support five-class sleep staging and respiratory-event scoring. A direct cardiorespiratory pathway preserves respiratory information for event detection, and the resulting scores additionally support patient-level apnea--hypopnea index estimation.

We evaluate the framework using patient-independent protocols across stroke, healthy-sleep, and sleep-disorder populations. The primary analysis uses 99 unique iSLEEPS recordings after a duplicate-record audit, supported by matched staging and respiratory baselines and controlled encoder comparisons. Controlled experiments further establish the contribution of embedding alignment to sleep-adapted encoder integration. To examine applicability beyond the development population, the framework is separately evaluated on Sleep-EDF and ISRUC-Sleep. Additional experiments assess transfer of the iSLEEPS-trained model to independent cohorts without retraining. Together, these evaluations investigate both the methodological contribution of representation-consistent adaptation and the applicability of joint physiological modeling across different sleep populations.

The principal contributions of this study are threefold:

\begin{enumerate}

\item Representation-consistent integration of sleep-adapted EEG features. We address the mismatch arising when an EEG encoder adapted on training patients supplies representations to a downstream model evaluated on unseen patients. A cross-fitting and embedding-alignment procedure integrates out-of-sample neural representations into a common deployment space, enabling the downstream framework to benefit from sleep-specific adaptation without relying on in-sample encoder outputs during training.

\item Task-specialized multimodal modeling of post-stroke sleep physiology. We develop a unified framework for sleep-stage classification and respiratory-event detection that combines neural and cardiorespiratory representations through shared temporal modeling while retaining task-specific respiratory information. This design enables concurrent characterization of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbances in subacute ischemic stroke, with patient-level AHI estimation as an additional output.

\item Cross-cohort evidence against matched baselines. Through patient-independent evaluations against matched staging and respiratory baselines, we establish the performance of the integrated framework on stroke, healthy-sleep, and sleep-disorder cohorts. Independent-cohort experiments further assess the transfer of the learned representations beyond the stroke population.

\end{enumerate}

The remainder of this paper presents the related literature, datasets, proposed methodology, experimental evaluation, and discussion of the findings.

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Automatic Sleep Staging}

Automatic sleep staging has progressed from feature-based classifiers to deep models that learn
temporal and spectral representations directly from PSG signals. DeepSleepNet combines
convolutional feature extraction with recurrent sequence modeling~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet},
SeqSleepNet formulates staging as sequence-to-sequence classification~\cite{phan2019seqsleepnet},
AttnSleep uses multi-resolution convolution and attention~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}, TinySleepNet
reduces model complexity while retaining recurrent modeling of stage
transitions~\cite{supratak2020tinysleepnet}, U-Time treats staging as dense time-series
segmentation~\cite{perslev2019utime}, and XSleepNet combines raw-signal and spectral
representations~\cite{phan2021xsleepnet}. SleepTransformer, L-SeqSleepNet, ZleepAnlystNet,
FlexSleepTransformer and Mamba-based models extend the range of temporal models, training strategies
and channel configurations~\cite{sleeptransformer2022,lseqsleepnet2023,jirakittayakorn2024zleep,guo2024flexsleep,gu2023mamba,bitmamsleep2024}.

In parallel, large pretrained EEG models such as LaBraM, CBraMod, EEGPT, and BIOT provide
representations learned from substantially larger collections of neural
recordings~\cite{labram2024,cbramod2025,eegpt2024,biot2023}. Such encoders are typically used
frozen or fine-tuned end to end for a single task. Using a fine-tuned encoder as the input of a
second model trained on the same patients raises the requirement addressed by cross-fitting, namely
that the second model be trained on out-of-sample first-stage outputs~\cite{chernozhukov2018dml}.
Most of these developments have been established on healthy or general sleep datasets, and
performance can change substantially across cohorts because acquisition protocols, channel
configurations, patient characteristics, and sleep physiology differ~\cite{guillot2023transfer}.

\subsection{Multimodal, Joint and Respiratory Analysis}

U-Sleep was developed to operate across different EEG and EOG derivations and multiple sleep
cohorts~\cite{perslev2021usleep}, and other multimodal approaches combine neural, ocular, muscular,
cardiac, or respiratory signals through early fusion, modality-specific encoders, or higher-level
feature fusion. Representation learning has also been extended across PSG modalities, including
SleepFM~\cite{sleepfm2026}, and cross-scale representations, self-supervised learning, and
standardized benchmarks have broadened the range of models evaluated across
datasets~\cite{csbrain2025,sleepyland2026,estevan2025ssl}.

Automated analysis of sleep-disordered breathing has largely developed as a separate task. Apnea and
hypopnea detectors commonly use airflow, thoracic and abdominal effort, oxygen saturation, and
ECG-derived measures; oxygen desaturation also forms part of the clinical criteria used in hypopnea
scoring~\cite{aasm2023manual}, and end-to-end models include detection from a single
respiratory-effort belt~\cite{nassi2022effortbelt}. Joint modeling of sleep stage and sleep-related
disorders has received less attention. Biswal \emph{et al.}~\cite{biswal2018expert} trained a
recurrent--convolutional network to score sleep stages, apnea events, and limb movements from
clinical PSG; Huttunen \emph{et al.}~\cite{huttunen2023simultaneous} trained a single network for
simultaneous sleep-stage and respiratory-event scoring across 877 clinical recordings, with input
configurations ranging from pulse oximetry to a broader PSG montage; and multi-task approaches have
since been investigated in other clinical populations~\cite{multitask2025elderly}. These studies
demonstrate that the two outputs can be learned within a common model, but they have primarily been
evaluated in suspected-apnea, elderly, or general sleep populations.

\subsection{Sleep and Sleep-Disordered Breathing After Stroke}

Sleep disturbances after stroke have been associated with neurological and functional
recovery~\cite{bassetti2006sleep,denis2024sleep,tellenbach2023brainstem}, and alterations in sleep
spindles, slow-wave activity, and related electrophysiological patterns have been reported after
cerebrovascular
injury~\cite{gottselig2002stroke,fernandez2019spindles,simpson2023laterality,facchin2020slowwaves,zheng2025spindles};
quantitative EEG measures such as the brain symmetry index have also been examined in relation to
hemispheric injury~\cite{anastasi2017bsi}. These changes make stroke sleep a distinct setting for
models whose representations were developed predominantly on non-stroke populations. The iSLEEPS
dataset introduced a dedicated PSG resource for subacute ischemic stroke and reported baseline
sleep-staging performance~\cite{maiti2026isleeps}, and subsequent work has examined deep staging
models on this cohort~\cite{bose2026gap,alhazmi2026sleepstagenet}. Existing stroke-specific studies
have therefore concentrated on sleep-stage classification, and joint epoch-level modeling of sleep
stage and respiratory disturbance remains insufficiently characterized in this population.

\section{Datasets and Preprocessing}
\label{sec:data}

Four publicly available polysomnography datasets are used in this study (Table~\ref{tab:datasets}):
iSLEEPS~\cite{maiti2026isleeps}, Sleep-EDF
~\cite{kemp2000sleepedf,goldberger2000physionet}, ISRUC-Sleep
~\cite{khalighi2016isruc} with its three subgroups, and the St Vincent's
University Hospital / University College Dublin sleep apnea database (UCD)
~\cite{ucddb2007}. iSLEEPS is the primary cohort used for model development and
patient-independent evaluation. Sleep-EDF is used as a healthy-sleep reference
for implementation checks, pretraining of a transferred baseline, and
subject-independent evaluation of the staging model outside the stroke cohort. ISRUC-Sleep provides
independent sleep-disorder and healthy cohorts with both sleep-stage and
respiratory-event annotations; it is used both to train and evaluate MM-Net
under subject-independent cross-validation and to test the iSLEEPS-trained model
without retraining. UCD provides a further sleep-apnea cohort recorded on a
different acquisition system and is used for transfer only. No external cohort is
used to select the reported iSLEEPS model configuration.

\subsection{iSLEEPS: Primary Stroke Cohort}

The iSLEEPS dataset contains overnight PSG from 100 patients with subacute
ischemic stroke~\cite{maiti2026isleeps}. The released cohort has a mean age of
$50.5$ years and includes 77 male and 23 female participants. Sleep is scored
into the five AASM stages: Wake (W), N1, N2, N3, and rapid-eye-movement (REM)
sleep. Respiratory events are also annotated throughout the recordings.

An audit performed before cross-validation identified the records of SN15 and
SN28 as byte-identical. Retaining both identifiers could place the same
recording in different patient-level folds. One member of the duplicate pair
was therefore removed before constructing the evaluation splits, leaving
$N=99$ patients and $92{,}560$ 30-s epochs for the analyses reported here.

The resulting sleep-stage distribution is shown in
Table~\ref{tab:dist}. N2 accounts for the largest proportion of epochs, and N1 and N3 are the least frequent stages. Respiratory-event onsets are marked in $16.0\%$ of all
epochs. At the patient level, the fraction of event-positive epochs ranges from
$0$ to $60.5\%$; 80 of the 99 patients have respiratory events in more than
$5\%$ of their epochs, and the median burden is $13\%$
(Figure~\ref{fig:prev}).

\subsection{Sleep-EDF: Healthy-Sleep Reference Cohort}

Sleep-EDF provides the healthy-sleep reference~\cite{kemp2000sleepedf,goldberger2000physionet}.
The experiments use the Sleep Cassette subset corresponding to subjects 0--20, comprising 40
overnight recordings, with splits constructed at the subject level because individual subjects
contribute more than one night. Sleep-EDF serves to check the reimplemented staging architectures
in the healthy-sleep setting in which they were originally evaluated, to pretrain the transferred
baseline, and to evaluate the proposed staging model on an independent healthy-sleep cohort. The
database provides the EEG derivations Fpz-Cz and Pz-Oz but not the cardiorespiratory signal set or
respiratory-event annotations, so respiratory performance is not evaluated on Sleep-EDF.

\subsection{ISRUC-Sleep: Independent Sleep-Disorder Cohort}

ISRUC-Sleep provides the independent evaluation in populations with and without
sleep disorders~\cite{khalighi2016isruc}. Subgroup I contains $100$ patients with
sleep disorders recorded on one night each, subgroup II eight patients recorded
on two nights each, and subgroup III ten healthy adults. The recordings include
central and occipital EEG derivations corresponding to C4, C3, O2, and O1
referenced to the contralateral mastoid, two EOG derivations and chin EMG,
together with oronasal airflow, nasal pressure, thoracic and abdominal effort,
SpO$_2$, and ECG, and annotations of obstructive, central, and mixed apneas and
hypopneas; the annotations of the first scorer are used. Summed effort is formed
as the sum of the thoracic and abdominal traces, and pulse as the beat-to-beat
heart rate derived from ECG R-peaks, so that every iSLEEPS input has an ISRUC
counterpart. In $30$ of the $126$ ISRUC recordings the EDF header declares the
EEG and EOG range as $\pm25\,\mu$V, which places their N3 peak-to-peak amplitude
at $9$--$23\,\mu$V against $98$--$283\,\mu$V in the remaining recordings and below
the $75\,\mu$V that defines N3; these channels are restored by a factor of ten, a
correction decided from the header alone. One subgroup-I recording stores
monopolar EEG and EOG, from which the mastoid-referenced derivations are formed.

ISRUC-Sleep therefore supports evaluation of every output of the proposed
framework: sleep-stage prediction, respiratory-event prediction and, in subgroup I,
AHI estimation across the full severity range.

\subsection{UCD: Independent Sleep-Apnea Cohort}

The UCD database~\cite{ucddb2007,goldberger2000physionet} contains overnight PSG
from $25$ adults referred for suspected sleep-disordered breathing, with annotated
AHI between $1.2$ and $81.2$ events/h. It records C3 and C4 EEG only, which also
supply the occipital inputs, two EOG channels, chin EMG, ECG, oronasal airflow,
thoracic, abdominal and summed effort, SpO$_2$ and pulse, and its scored apneas
and hypopneas carry onset times. The EDF headers of this database carry no physical
unit, so EEG, EOG and EMG are scaled per recording to the median whole-night
amplitude of iSLEEPS, using no labels. UCD is used only for transfer.

\subsection{Signal Selection and Harmonization}

The iSLEEPS recordings contain 22 physiological and auxiliary traces. Fifteen
signals are retained for modeling and divided into neural and
cardiorespiratory groups. The neural group contains four EEG derivations
(C4:M1, C3:M2, O2:M1, and O1:M2), two EOG derivations (E1:M2 and E2:M2), and
the submental EMG. The cardiorespiratory group contains ECG, oronasal thermal
airflow, nasal pressure, thoracic effort, abdominal effort, summed respiratory
effort, SpO$_2$, and pulse.

Signals associated with periodic limb movement, snoring, plethysmography, body
position, ambient light, and device operation are not included in the model.
The snore channel is additionally excluded from respiratory prediction because
it is closely related to the airway information used during respiratory-event
assessment. Twelve iSLEEPS recordings lack at least one selected
cardiorespiratory channel. These patients are retained, and unavailable inputs
are zero-filled. The same
convention is used when a selected signal is unavailable in an external
dataset.


\subsection{Preprocessing and Epoch Labels}

All sleep-stage predictions are defined on 30-s epochs. For iSLEEPS and
ISRUC-Sleep, the respiratory annotations are mapped to the same epoch grid. An
epoch is assigned a positive respiratory label if a scored respiratory event begins
within it and a negative label otherwise. The primary
respiratory task is therefore prediction of respiratory-event onset within
a 30-s epoch, and the patient-level AHI is derived from these predictions
(Section~\ref{sec:ahimethod}).

iSLEEPS retains only the epochs scored as a sleep stage, so every cardiorespiratory
signal and every respiratory-event label is cut at the true recording time of each
retained epoch, keeping it on the same timeline as the EEG. The annotated AHI is the
number of apneas and hypopneas starting in scored sleep per hour of scored sleep.

The EEG, EOG, and EMG signals are resampled to $100$\,Hz for the engineered
descriptors of Section~\ref{sec:features}, and the four EEG and two EOG derivations
are resampled independently to $200$\,Hz for the LaBraM encoder, which does not
receive the EMG. The eight cardiorespiratory inputs are resampled to a common
$25$\,Hz grid by polyphase resampling and remain in their recorded physical units;
SpO$_2$, recorded at $4$\,Hz, is placed on the same grid without gaining temporal
resolution. Engineered features are standardized within each recording, which for
iSLEEPS is the patient, and the same per-recording procedure is applied to the
external datasets, from which no labels are used to refit parameters, select
thresholds, or adapt the reported iSLEEPS model.

\section{Proposed Method}
\label{sec:method}
\subsection{Problem Formulation}
A night is a sequence of $T$ epochs. For epoch $t$ we form a neural feature vector
$\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eeg}}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{388}$, formed by concatenating the $188$
engineered descriptors below with a $200$-dimensional embedding from LaBraM, a
pretrained EEG encoder~\cite{labram2024} adapted to sleep (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}), and a raw cardiorespiratory tensor
$\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{car}}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times750}$, the eight channels at $25$\,Hz.
The $14$ engineered cardiorespiratory descriptors defined below are used by the baseline
configuration against which the learned encoder is measured.

The encoder receives its input in the format used during pretraining. LaBraM fixes its input
length at $3000$ samples at $200$\,Hz; supplying the $100$\,Hz signal directly would compress time
by a factor of two, so that a $12$\,Hz spindle would appear at $24$\,Hz. Each $30$-s epoch is
therefore split into two $15$-s sub-windows that are encoded separately and mean-pooled. The four EEG derivations are presented
under the canonical names C4, C3, O2 and O1, the two EOG derivations as F7 and F8, the frontal
positions nearest the eyes, so that the encoder receives the ocular activity that separates REM
from Wake, and amplitudes are scaled to $\mu$V$/100$. We learn a subject-independent
map
\begin{equation}
f_\theta:\big(\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eeg}}_{1:T},\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{car}}_{1:T}\big)\ \longmapsto\
\big(\hat y^{\mathrm{stg}}_{1:T},\ \hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_{1:T}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\Delta^4=\{p\in\mathbb{R}^5:p_k\geq 0,\ \sum_k p_k=1\}$ is the four-simplex,
$\hat y^{\mathrm{stg}}_t\in\Delta^4$ is a distribution over the five stages and
$\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t\in[0,1]$ scores whether epoch $t$ contains a respiratory
event (Figure~\ref{fig:arch}). We write $\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t$ as a score and not as a
probability for the reason given in Section~\ref{sec:training}: the positive weighting in its
loss displaces the optimum away from the conditional event rate.

\subsection{Physiological Feature Representations}
\label{sec:features}
\paragraph{Neural features ($188$)}
Per channel we take absolute and relative band powers from the Welch spectrum $P(f)$ in delta
($0.5$--$4$), theta ($4$--$8$), alpha ($8$--$13$), sigma ($11$--$16$), and beta
($16$--$30$)\,Hz; the relative power in band $b$ is
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{RP}_b=\frac{\int_{f\in b}P(f)\,df}{\int_{0.5}^{30}P(f)\,df}.
\end{equation}
The conventional alpha and sigma bands overlap between $11$ and $13$\,Hz, so the five relative
powers are treated as correlated descriptors whose sum is unconstrained.
We add spectral entropy $H=-\sum_i p_i\log p_i$ with $p_i$ the normalized spectrum, the
$95\%$ spectral-edge frequency, the three Hjorth descriptors~\cite{hjorth1970eeg}, and
time-domain statistics. Sleep spindles are detected on the sigma-band analytic envelope,
the modulus of the analytic signal built from the Hilbert transform $\mathcal{H}$,
$e(t)=\lvert x_\sigma(t)+i\,\mathcal{H}\{x_\sigma\}(t)\rvert=\sqrt{x_\sigma(t)^2+\mathcal{H}\{x_\sigma\}(t)^2}$, with
density counted above a per-epoch threshold as a proxy for spindle density, and slow-wave
descriptors summarise slow-wave amplitude. Ocular-movement energy on the EOG and tonic EMG level
complete the set.

\paragraph{Cardiorespiratory features ($14$)}
From the seven cardiorespiratory channels we compute SpO$_2$ mean, minimum, variability, and
desaturation depth (median minus minimum); pulse mean and variability; ECG amplitude and
line-length; airflow amplitude and line-length; thoracic, abdominal, and summed effort
amplitudes; and the signed thoraco-abdominal synchrony
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{sync}_t=\mathrm{corr}(x^{\mathrm{tho}}_t,\ x^{\mathrm{abd}}_t),
\end{equation}
whose negative values mark the paradoxical motion of chest against abdomen in an obstructed
breath. These descriptors are read by the classical
respiratory baselines. Both feature sets are standardized per patient.

\paragraph{The learned cardiorespiratory encoder}
MM-Net does not read the $14$ descriptors. Its cardiorespiratory branch is a three-layer convolutional encoder over the raw $8\times750$ tensor, with wide kernels and aggressive downsampling because respiratory events are slow: at $25$\,Hz the first kernel of $25$ samples spans one second, and a hypopnea unfolds over $10$ to $30$\,s. Channel widths are
$48$, $96$ and $96$, each followed by batch normalisation and GELU, with max-pooling by four
after the first two. Mean and max pooling over time are concatenated and projected by a
linear layer, LayerNorm, GELU and dropout to the same
$64$ dimensions as the cardiorespiratory feature MLP it replaces, so the encoder is a drop-in replacement and every
other part of the network is unchanged. The branch holds $156{,}432$ trainable parameters
with eight input channels ($155{,}232$ with seven), is trained end-to-end with the rest of the
network, and adds no external dependency.

\subsection{Sleep-Adapted EEG Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}
\paragraph{Adaptation} Within each training fold, LaBraM-Base ($5.8$\,M parameters) is
fine-tuned with a linear staging head on single epochs of the fold's training patients, using
AdamW at $3\times10^{-5}$ for the encoder and $10^{-3}$ for the head, a one-cycle schedule
over three passes of the training epochs, batch size $64$ and mixed precision; the state with
the highest accuracy on the fold's validation patients is kept. Test patients never reach the
encoder.

\paragraph{Cross-fitting} An encoder fine-tuned on a patient embeds that patient in-sample. If the downstream model is trained on such embeddings and tested on out-of-sample ones, the two distributions differ, and the downstream model is fitted to representations of higher quality than those available at test time. Each fold's training patients are therefore split
into $K=3$ parts $P_1,P_2,P_3$, and an encoder $\phi_k$ is fine-tuned on the training patients
outside $P_k$; every training patient in $P_k$ is embedded by $\phi_k$, an encoder that never
saw it. The full-fold encoder $\phi$, fine-tuned on all training patients, embeds the
validation and test patients.

\paragraph{Alignment} Cross-fitting makes every embedding out of sample but places the
training patients in the spaces of $K$ different encoders, which do not coincide with the
space of $\phi$ used at test time. Each $\phi_k$ is mapped into the space of $\phi$ by a ridge
regression~\cite{hoerl1970ridge} fitted on the patients of $P_k$, for whom both embeddings
exist,
\begin{equation}
W_k=\arg\min_W\big\lVert(E^{\phi}_{P_k}-\mu^{\phi}_k)-(E^{\phi_k}_{P_k}-\mu^{\phi_k}_k)W\big\rVert_F^2
+\lambda n_k\lVert W\rVert_F^2,
\end{equation}
and the training embeddings become
$\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_t=(\mathbf{e}^{\phi_k}_t-\mu^{\phi_k}_k)W_k+\mu^{\phi}_k$, with $\lambda=0.01$
and $n_k$ the number of epochs in $P_k$. The map uses training patients only. Every training
embedding is thereby produced by an encoder that never saw its patient and expressed in the
coordinates the downstream model receives at test time; Section~\ref{sec:integration} shows that
the alignment is what allows the adapted encoder to benefit the downstream model.

\subsection{Two-Stream Encoders}
The neural stream is a two-layer perceptron with LayerNorm; the cardiorespiratory stream is the convolutional encoder described above, which emits a vector of the same width as the feature perceptron it replaced,
\begin{align}
e_t&=\mathrm{GELU}\!\big(\mathrm{LN}(W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}(\mathrm{LN}(W_1\mathbf{f}^{\mathrm{eeg}}_t))) \big)\in\mathbb{R}^{128},\\
c_t&=\mathrm{CardioCNN}\big(\mathbf{x}^{\mathrm{car}}_t\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{64}.
\end{align}
LayerNorm is used in place of BatchNorm because the recurrent windows are short and mix patients
within a batch.

\subsection{Fusion}
Two fusion operators were compared. Concatenation, used in the frozen-encoder configuration,
projects the joined embeddings back to the stream width,
\begin{equation}
z_t=\mathrm{GELU}\!\big(W_f\,[\,e_t\ ;\ c_t\,]\big)\in\mathbb{R}^{D},\qquad D=128.
\end{equation}
Cross-attention treats the two embeddings as tokens carrying learned modality-type
embeddings, refines them by four-head scaled dot-product attention so that each modality
reads the other, and projects the result to $z_t\in\mathbb{R}^{D}$. With uniform class weights
and the sleep-adapted encoder, cross-attention was selected on validation accuracy and is the
fusion of the final MM-Net (Table~\ref{tab:components}).

\subsection{Temporal Decoder and Two Heads}
Sleep is sequential and respiratory events cluster, so the fused vectors of an $L=20$ epoch
window ($10$ minutes) are read by a two-layer bidirectional LSTM. Training draws windows at a
stride of $L/2$ with masked zero-padding at the ends of each night; inference uses disjoint
windows, so every epoch is scored exactly once, and hidden states are not carried between
windows. Each direction runs the standard recurrence
\begin{align}
i_t&=\sigma(W_i[z_t,h_{t-1}]), & f_t&=\sigma(W_f[z_t,h_{t-1}]),\nonumber\\
o_t&=\sigma(W_o[z_t,h_{t-1}]), & g_t&=\tanh(W_g[z_t,h_{t-1}]),\\
s_t&=f_t\odot s_{t-1}+i_t\odot g_t, & h_t&=o_t\odot\tanh(s_t),\nonumber
\end{align}
and the forward and backward states of the $256$-unit layers are concatenated into
$h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{512}$. The
staging head is a linear map $\hat y^{\mathrm{stg}}_t=\mathrm{softmax}(W_s h_t)$. The
respiratory head reads $h_t$ together with a direct copy of the per-epoch cardiorespiratory
embedding $c_t$,
\begin{equation}
\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t=\sigma\!\big(W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_1[h_t;c_t])\big).
\end{equation}
MM-Net holds $2.85$ million trainable parameters. The sleep-adapted encoder that supplies
the $200$-dimensional neural embedding ($5.8$ million parameters) is fine-tuned in a separate
stage per fold (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}) and run once to cache the embeddings, so it is not
part of this count.

\subsection{Training and Decoding}
\label{sec:training}
The two tasks are trained together with equal weight, $\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{stg}}+\lambda\,\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{apn}}$ with $\lambda=1$, combining a staging cross-entropy with class weights $w_k$ and a positive-weighted respiratory cross-entropy,
\begin{align}
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{stg}}&=-\sum_t\sum_{k}w_k\,y^{\mathrm{stg}}_{t,k}\log\hat y^{\mathrm{stg}}_{t,k},\\
\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{apn}}&=-\sum_t\big[\pi\,a_t\log\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t+(1-a_t)\log(1-\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t)\big].
\end{align}
The final MM-Net uses uniform staging weights ($w_k=1$), selected on validation patients
together with the encoder (Table~\ref{tab:components}); the frozen-encoder configuration uses the
square root of inverse class frequency,
$w_k\propto\sqrt{N/(K n_k)}$ for $K=5$ classes with $n_k$ examples, which corrects the scarce
N1 and N3 stages. In both, the respiratory term is
positive-weighted by $\pi=n_-/n_+$ to match the $16\%$ event rate, which on this corpus is
$\pi\approx5.3$.

Against a true conditional rate $p$, the weighted binary term attains its minimum at
$q^{\star}=\pi p/[\pi p+(1-p)]>p$, so the respiratory output is treated as a score and not as a
calibrated probability; the displacement is monotone, so AUC and average precision are
unaffected.

At inference the staging
posteriors are smoothed by a Viterbi pass whose transition matrix $A^{\mathrm H}$
and prior are counted from the training labels~\cite{chen2024hmm},
\begin{equation}
\delta_t(j)=\max_i\big[\delta_{t-1}(i)+\log A^{\mathrm H}_{ij}\big]+\log\hat y^{\mathrm{stg}}_t(j),
\end{equation}
recovered by back-tracking, which restores the natural persistence of sleep. Because the network
score, and not a generative likelihood $\log P(\mathbf{x}_t\mid s_t=j)$, occupies the emission
slot, the pass is a transition-regularised discriminative decoder that acts as a transition prior
on the output sequence; the respiratory head is left unsmoothed. Optimization
uses AdamW with cosine annealing and early stopping on a held-out set of patients.

\subsection{AHI Estimation}
\label{sec:ahimethod}
Because $\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t$ scores event onsets, its sum over the epochs decoded as sleep
estimates the number of events, and dividing by the decoded sleep time gives a rate,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{AHI}}=k\cdot\frac{\sum_{t:\hat s_t\neq\mathrm{W}}\hat y^{\mathrm{apn}}_t}{\tfrac{30}{3600}\,\lvert\{t:\hat s_t\neq\mathrm{W}\}\rvert}.
\end{equation}
The positive weighting of the respiratory loss inflates the scores
(Section~\ref{sec:training}), so a single scale $k$ is fitted in each fold as the median ratio
of annotated to raw estimate over the fold's validation patients and applied unchanged to its
test patients. For transfer to another cohort, $k$ is the median of the ten iSLEEPS fold
values ($0.459$), and nothing is refitted on the external cohort. Severity follows the AASM
bands ($<5$, $5$--$15$, $15$--$30$ and $\geq30$ events/h).

\section{Experiments}
\label{sec:results}
\subsection{Protocol and Metrics}
All results use ten-fold, patient-independent cross-validation: no patient appears in both
training and test, and within each training split a disjoint set of patients is held out for
early stopping and model selection. Staging is scored by accuracy, macro-F1
$\tfrac{1}{K}\sum_k\mathrm{F1}_k$, and Cohen's $\kappa$. The respiratory task is scored by the
area under the ROC curve (AUC) and average precision (AP), which suits the $16\%$ event rate,
and the AHI estimate by Spearman's $\rho$, the mean absolute error (MAE), severity-band accuracy
and linearly weighted $\kappa$.

MM-Net and its encoder configurations are trained with three seeds ($42$, $1$, $7$) on the same
folds. The single-model figure is the mean over the thirty fold-values, ten folds by three seeds; the
three-seed figure averages the softmax outputs of the three seeds before decoding. Every number
is a mean over the ten folds with its standard deviation across folds. Paired comparisons use
the two-sided Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the ten fold means, because the three seeds within a
fold train and test on the same patients; with ten folds the smallest attainable $p$ is $0.002$,
and Holm correction is applied within each family of comparisons.

The frozen-encoder configuration, the initial configuration of the same architecture, serves as
the reference for the encoder analyses. It shares the streams, decoder and heads of MM-Net but
reads a frozen LaBraM embedding of the EEG, seven cardiorespiratory channels, concatenation
fusion and square-root class weights. The encoder, fusion operator and class weighting of the
final MM-Net were selected on validation patients only.

\subsection{Staging Benchmark}
Table~\ref{tab:bench} compares MM-Net with deep staging architectures retrained on the same
folds. These reach $0.60$--$0.72$ staging accuracy, whereas MM-Net reaches $\mmAccS$ as a single
model and $\mmAcc$ as a three-seed average, with macro-F1 $\mmMf$ and $\kappa$ $\mmK$. As a
single model, MM-Net is higher in accuracy by $+0.036$ (corpus LSTM) to $+0.157$
(Huttunen~\emph{et al.}) and is higher in nine or ten of the ten folds for every comparison; all
thirteen differences remain significant after Holm correction (Holm $p\leq0.025$). The three-seed
MM-Net is ahead of each of the eight reimplemented and ported architectures in all ten folds
(Holm $p=0.016$, the minimum attainable for eight comparisons). Differences in macro-F1 and
$\kappa$ are larger than those in accuracy, consistent with their reduced dependence on the
majority classes. The sleep-adapted encoder used on its own stages single epochs at $0.732$,
already above every retrained architecture; within MM-Net, together with the engineered
descriptors, the cardiorespiratory stream and the temporal decoder, it yields $\mmAccS$, a further
$0.024$ in eight folds of ten ($p=0.014$).

The closest task-matched baseline is the joint model of Huttunen~\emph{et al.}
\cite{huttunen2023simultaneous}, ported from the authors' released implementation and evaluated
on the same folds. It stages at $0.599$, and MM-Net is ahead in all ten folds (Wilcoxon
$p=0.002$). The port uses four of the five signals of the fullest original configuration,
excludes nasal pressure, maps the original per-second three-class respiratory output to the 30-s
binary target used here, and uses a single EEG derivation. On ISRUC-I, where both models are
trained and evaluated on the same respiratory labels, MM-Net is also ahead on respiratory
detection (Section~\ref{sec:external}).

The CNN-ResNet18 result of~\cite{bose2026gap} is quoted under that study's $N=100$ protocol,
which includes the duplicate recording excluded here; the same study's SE-ResNet-18\,+\,BiLSTM,
retrained from the released implementation on our folds, reaches $0.696$ against the published
$0.747$, and Table~\ref{tab:bench} uses the same-fold rerun. The corpus LSTM appears as our
reimplementation on the same folds. Each reimplementation is checked against the Sleep-EDF figure
its own paper publishes (Table~\ref{tab:reimpl}); because those figures follow each paper's
protocol, the check is one of consistency. U-Time is compared in macro-F1 because its paper does
not report Sleep-EDF accuracy, and its published value uses $39$ recordings under twenty-fold
cross-validation against $40$ recordings under ten folds here. The Healthy column gives each
model's subject-independent accuracy on Sleep-EDF, where MM-Net reaches $0.864$ (macro-F1
$0.791$, $\kappa$ $0.810$), the highest healthy-sleep accuracy in the table; Sleep-EDF carries
none of the cardiorespiratory channels the model reads, so this figure exercises the neural
stream alone. Dispersion is reported wherever it is defined: across five folds for the
Sleep-EDF-pretrained StagingSeqNet, across the ten fold values of a single seed for the retrained
and reimplemented architectures, and across the thirty fold values, ten folds by three seeds, for
AttnSleep and MM-Net.

Errors concentrate on N1 (Table~\ref{tab:perclass}, Figure~\ref{fig:confusion}). MM-Net recovers Wake, N2 and REM at F1
$0.79$--$0.82$ and N3 at $0.72$, whereas N1, the transient stage between wake and sleep, reaches

\subsection{Neural and Cardiorespiratory Representations}
\label{sec:repr}
Before the encoder was adapted, the representation of each stream was compared in the
frozen-encoder configuration. Each block of Table~\ref{tab:repr} varies one representation and
holds the other stream at its engineered-feature baseline, over the same three seeds and ten
folds, with Holm correction within the block and every test on the ten fold means; no single
row is therefore the full model.

On the neural side, a frozen pretrained encoder complements the expert features without
replacing them. Used alone, the frozen encoder is significantly \emph{worse} than the $188$
features ($-0.029$, Holm $p=0.008$): pretraining on healthy scalp EEG does not by itself
substitute for physiology chosen for this task. Concatenated with the features it adds $+0.008$
accuracy (Holm $p=0.006$), and a second pretrained encoder on top of the first adds nothing
further ($-0.004$, $p=0.56$). A generic pretrained representation therefore contributes little
beyond the expert descriptors, which motivates adapting the encoder to sleep
(Section~\ref{sec:integration}).

On the cardiorespiratory side, respiratory discrimination rises as the representation of the
same signals is enriched. A random nonlinear expansion of the $14$ engineered descriptors
recovers $0.018$ AUC from added capacity alone, indicating that the summary features leave
nonlinear structure unrepresented; a random linear projection of the raw waveform adds a
further $0.016$; and the learned convolutional encoder reaches $0.782$, $0.084$ above the
engineered features. Frozen MOMENT time-series encoders~\cite{goswami2024moment} reach $0.808$
with pretrained and $0.795$ with randomly initialised weights. The learned encoder is retained in
MM-Net as the compact implementation: it holds about $0.16$ million trainable parameters,
trained end to end with the rest of the network, against the $341$ million frozen parameters of
MOMENT.

\subsection{Integrating the Sleep-Adapted Encoder}
\label{sec:integration}
Table~\ref{tab:components} builds MM-Net from the frozen-encoder configuration one change at a
time, every row on the same folds and seeds. Uniform class weights and cross-attention fusion
contribute $+0.009$ together for the single model. The largest change comes from the encoder.
Adapting LaBraM to sleep raises its own single-epoch accuracy from $0.647$ to $0.709$, in all ten
folds, yet a sleep-adapted encoder whose training embeddings are cross-fitted but not aligned
moves the three-seed accuracy of the downstream model by only $+0.002$ ($p=0.63$) and falls below
the frozen encoder in five folds of ten, by up to $0.023$ in the fold where the cross-fitted and
full encoders agree least (embedding cosine similarity $0.53$). Aligning the embedding spaces
turns the same encoder into a gain of $+0.014$ ($0.755\rightarrow0.769$, $p=0.020$), and of
$+0.015$ for the single model in nine folds of ten ($p=0.027$). The downstream benefit of
adaptation therefore appears once the training embeddings share the coordinates of the encoder
used at test time.

The complete MM-Net adds three elements that serve REM and the respiratory head more than overall
staging: the ocular channels in the encoder, which raise the encoder's own accuracy from $0.709$
to $0.732$, nasal pressure, and the epoch alignment of Section~\ref{sec:data}. With all three,
staging is $\mmAcc$; an EEG-only encoder in the same model gives $0.765$, which is
indistinguishable ($p=0.38$), and MM-Net uses the EEG and EOG encoder in every cohort of this
study. The same integration carries over to an independent cohort: on ISRUC-I the complete model
is ahead of the frozen-encoder configuration in nine folds of ten ($+0.018$, $p=0.004$;
Section~\ref{sec:external}). Figure~\ref{fig:tsne} shows what adaptation changes in the
representation: held-out epochs that the frozen encoder spreads across one mixed cloud form
stage-specific clusters after adaptation, with Wake, REM and N3 separated (silhouette by stage
$-0.045\rightarrow0.047$).

\subsection{Respiratory-Event Detection}
\label{sec:noflow}
Table~\ref{tab:resp} compares the respiratory output with established detectors on the same folds
and epoch labels. A clinical desaturation rule reaches $0.595$ AUC, and logistic regression and
gradient boosting on the $14$ engineered cardiorespiratory descriptors reach $0.603$ and $0.673$;
a random classifier scores $0.160$ AP, the event prevalence. Two raw-signal detectors, each a
per-epoch convolutional encoder over the $25$\,Hz waveform followed by a bidirectional LSTM across
$20$-epoch windows and trained with the same labels, folds, early-stopping rule and positive-class
weighting, read summed respiratory effort alone, matching the input of
Nassi~\emph{et al.}~\cite{nassi2022effortbelt} ($0.602$ AUC), or all seven raw cardiorespiratory
channels ($0.733$; one seed each). MM-Net reaches $\mmAuc$ AUC and $\mmAp$ AP, $0.152$ AUC and
$0.197$ AP above gradient boosting. Detection is strongest on the most severe events: $0.810$ AUC
on hypopneas, which form $82\%$ of event epochs, $0.898$ on obstructive and $0.946$ on central
apneas. Read at fixed sensitivity on the pooled held-out predictions (Figure~\ref{fig:rocpr}),
MM-Net holds $0.70$ specificity at $0.80$ sensitivity and $0.53$ at $0.90$.

\subsection{AHI Estimation}
\label{sec:ahi}
Summed over decoded sleep, the respiratory scores estimate the AHI
(Section~\ref{sec:ahimethod}) with Spearman $\rho=\mmRho$ against the annotated index over the
$99$ test patients, a mean absolute error of $10.4$ events/h, and $54\%$ of patients in the
correct AASM severity band (weighted $\kappa$ $0.45$; Table~\ref{tab:ahi}, Figure~\ref{fig:ahi}). The clinical AHI
recorded with the corpus agrees with the annotated index at $\rho=0.687$, and against that
clinical index MM-Net reaches $\rho=0.633$. In the expected-count formulation, summing the onset
scores over decoded sleep counts closely spaced events separately, whereas thresholding the scores
into runs would merge them, and normalising by decoded sleep time matches the denominator of the
clinical index. A dedicated event detector, a one-dimensional U-Net
with a recurrent bottleneck operating at one-second resolution on the raw cardiorespiratory
signals, reaches $\rho=0.669$; averaged with MM-Net it lowers the error to $9.7$ events/h.
Staging remains stable across sleep-disordered-breathing severity: accuracy is $0.784$ in patients
with a normal clinical AHI and $0.754$, $0.764$ and $0.758$ in the mild, moderate and severe
groups, with no significant ordered trend (Jonckheere--Terpstra $p=0.20$;
Table~\ref{tab:severity}), so the model stages the most severely affected patients nearly as well
as those without sleep-disordered breathing.

\subsection{External Validation on Independent Cohorts}
\label{sec:external}
Ten-fold cross-validation is patient-independent but remains within one corpus, one centre
and one acquisition protocol. MM-Net is therefore evaluated in two further settings: trained
and tested on independent cohorts under subject-independent cross-validation, with the encoder
adapted, cross-fitted and aligned inside each fold exactly as on iSLEEPS; and trained on all
$99$ iSLEEPS patients and applied unchanged to external cohorts, with no re-fitted parameter,
threshold or transition matrix (per-recording standardisation is applied, as in training).

\paragraph{Trained and tested on ISRUC-Sleep} ISRUC-I is evaluated with ten
subject-independent folds of $80$ training, $10$ validation and $10$ test subjects, and
ISRUC-II and III together with six folds that keep both nights of a subject on the same side.
On ISRUC-I MM-Net reaches $0.803$ accuracy, macro-F1 $0.765$ and $\kappa$ $0.744$
(Table~\ref{tab:isruccv}). It is ahead of the frozen-encoder configuration in nine folds of ten
($+0.018$, $p=0.004$), and the gain extends to respiratory detection
($0.872\rightarrow0.889$ AUC) and to the AHI ($\rho$ $0.523\rightarrow0.590$, mean absolute error
$6.6$ events/h). As on iSLEEPS, MM-Net is ahead of its sleep-adapted encoder used alone ($0.792$;
$+0.012$ in eight folds of ten, $p=0.014$). The reimplemented baselines, retrained on MM-Net's
ISRUC-I folds with three seeds, reach $0.691$ to $0.778$; MM-Net is ahead of each of them in nine
or ten folds of ten, by $+0.025$ over the corpus LSTM to $+0.112$ over Huttunen~et~al. (Holm
$p=0.018$, the minimum for nine comparisons), and ahead of Huttunen~et~al., the only other joint
model, on respiratory detection as well ($0.889$ against $0.863$ AUC). On ISRUC-II and III MM-Net
reaches $0.725$ over $26$ nights and $0.779$ on the ten healthy subjects of ISRUC-III.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

The central finding of this study concerns how a pretrained EEG encoder, once adapted to the
target cohort, enters a downstream model. Adapting LaBraM to sleep raises its own staging accuracy
from $0.647$ to $0.709$, yet the adapted encoder adds nothing measurable to the downstream model
until its training embeddings are cross-fitted and mapped into the space of the deployed encoder
(Table~\ref{tab:components}). The failure mode, a downstream model trained on embeddings whose
coordinates differ from those it receives at test time, may apply to any pipeline that fine-tunes
a foundation model and then trains a second model on its outputs, and the remedy costs $K$
additional fine-tuning runs and a ridge regression per part. The aligned encoder separates Wake,
REM and N3 into distinct clusters (Figure~\ref{fig:tsne}), and with it MM-Net stages iSLEEPS at
$\mmAcc$ ($\kappa=\mmK$), ahead of every architecture retrained on the same folds. The integration
carries over to ISRUC-I, where the complete model is ahead of the frozen-encoder configuration in
nine folds of ten.

The representation experiments place this result in context. Engineered descriptors form a
strong standalone neural representation, and a frozen pretrained encoder is worse on its own and
adds only $+0.008$ when combined with them (Table~\ref{tab:repr}). Sleep-specific physiological
descriptors and large-scale EEG pretraining therefore encode partly different information, and
the generic pretrained representation contributes little until it is adapted to the target
population; adapted and aligned, the encoder carries MM-Net to $\mmAcc$. This combination suits
the present setting, where the supervised stroke cohort is modest in size and fully end-to-end
representation learning would place a greater burden on the available data. On the
cardiorespiratory side the gain comes from preserving the temporal structure of the raw
waveforms: the learned encoder is $0.084$ AUC above the engineered descriptors, and the
learned stream lifts respiratory detection to $\mmAuc$ AUC in the final model.

The same model delivers the respiratory output and the AHI. Huttunen \emph{et al.}
\cite{huttunen2023simultaneous} demonstrated simultaneous sleep staging and respiratory-event
scoring in a large suspected-obstructive-sleep-apnea cohort. Under the common iSLEEPS evaluation
their architecture reaches $0.599$ staging accuracy against $\mmAccS$ for MM-Net, and on ISRUC-I,
trained and evaluated on the same labels, $0.691$ staging accuracy and $0.863$ respiratory AUC
against $0.803$ and $0.889$. On iSLEEPS the respiratory output reaches $\mmAuc$ AUC, $0.152$ above
gradient boosting on engineered descriptors and $0.092$ above a raw-signal network on seven
cardiorespiratory channels, and it is strongest on obstructive and central apneas. Summed over decoded sleep,
the same scores estimate the AHI with $\rho=\mmRho$ against the annotated index, so a single model
characterizes both sleep architecture and respiratory disturbance in stroke PSG.

The respiratory output is trained at the 30-s temporal scale of staging, as a score for the onset
of an event within each epoch, and the AHI follows from it without a separate counting rule. The
expected-count formulation counts closely spaced events separately and normalises by the decoded
sleep time, as the clinical index does, and its agreement with the annotated index
($\rho=\mmRho$) exceeds the agreement of the clinical index recorded with the corpus
($\rho=0.687$). Staging accuracy is $0.784$ in patients with a normal AHI and remains at
$0.754$--$0.764$ in the mild, moderate and severe groups, without a significant trend, so the model
stages the patients with the greatest respiratory burden nearly as well as those without it. Sleep
architecture and respiratory disturbance remain distinct prediction problems at the signal level
while sharing clinically relevant structure at the level of the overnight recording, which
supports their analysis within a common framework in a stroke population where both processes
shape the physiological state of the patient during sleep.

Performance depends strongly on the population in which a model is evaluated. Deep architectures
retrained on iSLEEPS reach approximately $0.60$--$0.72$ accuracy, against $0.79$--$0.84$ for the
same architectures on Sleep-EDF, a decrease of approximately $0.10$ to $0.15$ for each model;
because population, montage, and acquisition conditions change together, this difference is most
appropriately read as a substantial shift between conventional healthy-sleep data and subacute
stroke PSG. MM-Net reaches $0.864$ on Sleep-EDF, $0.803$ on ISRUC-I and
$\mmAcc$ within the stroke cohort. Trained on ISRUC-I, MM-Net reaches $0.803$ accuracy and $0.889$
respiratory AUC and is ahead of every retrained baseline on the same folds. Trained on iSLEEPS alone
and applied without retraining, it detects respiratory events at $0.833$ and $0.841$ AUC on ISRUC-I
and on UCD, an apnea cohort recorded on a different system, against $\mmAuc$ in-cohort, and
estimates the AHI with $\rho=0.631$ and $0.653$. The learned representations are therefore not
confined to a single recording environment. Nasal pressure was the input whose contribution
depended most on the acquisition system, and the seven-channel variant is the appropriate choice
for transfer.

For subacute ischemic-stroke PSG, MM-Net provides sleep staging, respiratory-event detection and
AHI estimation from one pass over the recording, built on a sleep-adapted EEG encoder whose
representations are brought into the downstream model through cross-fitting and alignment.

\section{Conclusion}

This study addresses the joint characterization of sleep architecture and respiratory disturbances in subacute ischemic stroke, a setting in which altered neural physiology and heterogeneous cardiorespiratory information challenge conventional sleep-analysis approaches. MM-Net demonstrates that sleep-adapted EEG representations can be integrated into a multimodal temporal framework through cross-fitting and embedding alignment, improving sleep-stage discrimination while supporting concurrent respiratory-event detection.

The findings establish two complementary aspects of the proposed approach. First, the integration of adapted neural representations provides a means of addressing the training--inference mismatch encountered when pretrained encoders are incorporated into downstream physiological models. Second, a single model with shared temporal modeling and task-specific prediction pathways delivers sleep staging, respiratory-event detection, and AHI estimation ahead of matched staging and respiratory baselines.

Patient-independent evaluation and experiments across independent sleep cohorts support the applicability of this approach beyond a single recording population. Together, these results establish a computational framework for joint analysis of neural and respiratory physiology after ischemic stroke, while providing a foundation for further investigation of representation consistency and multimodal generalization in clinical sleep analysis.
