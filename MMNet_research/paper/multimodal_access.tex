\begin{abstract}
Sleep-disordered breathing affects most patients after ischemic stroke and predicts poorer
recovery, yet automated sleep analysis reads the electroencephalogram and reports stages
alone. We present MM-Net, a multimodal multi-task network that reads the full polysomnogram
and returns, in one pass, the sleep stage of every 30-second epoch, a per-epoch
respiratory-event score and the patient's apnea--hypopnea index (AHI). Its neural stream
carries an EEG foundation encoder (LaBraM) fine-tuned on sleep with both EEG and ocular
channels, and we show that such an encoder improves the downstream sequence model only when its
training embeddings are cross-fitted and ridge-aligned to the deployed encoder; we introduce
that procedure. On iSLEEPS ($99$ stroke patients, ten-fold patient-independent
cross-validation) MM-Net stages sleep at $\mmAcc$ accuracy (Cohen's $\kappa$ $\mmK$), ahead of
all eight deep baselines retrained on the same folds in every fold (by $0.042$ to $0.164$; Holm
$p=0.016$), detects respiratory events at $\mmAuc$ AUC against $0.673$ for gradient boosting,
and estimates the AHI with Spearman $\rho=\mmRho$. The gains generalize. Trained and tested on
the $100$-patient ISRUC-I cohort, MM-Net reaches $\isrAcc$ accuracy ($\kappa$ $0.744$), with
ahead of its frozen-encoder configuration in nine of ten folds ($p=0.004$), and reaches
$0.864$ on healthy sleep (Sleep-EDF). Trained on iSLEEPS alone and applied unchanged, it stages
the $100$ ISRUC-I patients at $0.730$ and, on ISRUC-I and a $25$-patient apnea cohort, detects
respiratory events at $0.83$--$0.84$ AUC with AHI $\rho$ of $0.63$--$0.65$. MM-Net is, to our knowledge, the first model to deliver staging,
respiratory-event detection and AHI jointly for stroke polysomnography. Code and features are
released.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\PARstart{S}{leep} is scored in 30-second epochs into five stages, Wake (W), N1, N2, N3 and
rapid-eye-movement (REM) sleep, following the American Academy of Sleep Medicine (AASM)
rules~\cite{aasm2023manual}. Automated scoring has progressed from convolutional and recurrent
encoders~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet,phan2019seqsleepnet,supratak2020tinysleepnet} through
attention and sequence models~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep,phan2021xsleepnet,perslev2019utime} to
large self-supervised EEG foundation
models~\cite{labram2024,cbramod2025,eegpt2024,biot2023}, and on healthy sleepers these methods
reach about $85\%$ accuracy~\cite{phan2022survey,yang2026review}.

The patients who need sleep analysis most are not healthy sleepers. After an ischemic stroke,
sleep-disordered breathing is common and is tied to worse functional
recovery~\cite{bassetti2006sleep,denis2024sleep}, and the lesion itself distorts the spindles,
slow waves and muscle atonia that staging relies
on~\cite{gottselig2002stroke,fernandez2019spindles,facchin2020slowwaves}. A clinical
polysomnogram (PSG) records everything needed to address both problems at once, the EEG,
ocular and muscle activity, the electrocardiogram (ECG), airflow, thoracic and abdominal effort
and oxygen saturation (SpO$_2$), yet automated tools read the EEG and report the stage, leaving
the breathing disorder to manual scoring. On iSLEEPS~\cite{maiti2026isleeps}, the first
public stroke polysomnography corpus, the median patient carries a scored respiratory event
in $13\%$ of epochs (Fig.~\ref{fig:prev}), and architectures built on healthy sleep lose
$12$ to $20$ accuracy points~\cite{bose2026gap}.

We present MM-Net, a model that reads the whole polysomnogram of a stroke patient and returns
the three quantities a sleep clinician reports: the hypnogram, the respiratory events and the
apnea--hypopnea index (AHI). Its central technical element is the way it absorbs a pretrained
EEG foundation encoder. Fine-tuning LaBraM~\cite{labram2024} on sleep makes it a strong stager
on its own, but placing the fine-tuned encoder inside a sequence model naively yields nothing:
the encoder has seen its own training patients, so the embeddings the sequence model trains on
are in-sample while the ones it is tested on are not. We remove this mismatch by
\emph{cross-fitting} the training embeddings and \emph{aligning} each cross-fitted encoder to
the deployed one with a ridge map, and this is what turns a stronger encoder into a stronger
system.

Our contributions are:
\begin{enumerate}
\item \label{c:method}\textbf{Cross-fitted, aligned foundation-encoder integration.} We
fine-tune LaBraM on EEG and ocular channels and integrate it into a multimodal sequence model
through cross-fitting and ridge alignment (Section~\ref{sec:encoder}). Without alignment the
fine-tuned encoder adds $+0.002$ accuracy ($p=0.63$); with it, $+0.014$ ($p=0.020$), on the
same folds with every other component fixed (Table~\ref{tab:components}). On ISRUC-I the
complete model gains $+0.018$ over the frozen-encoder configuration in nine of ten folds
($p=0.004$).

\item \label{c:stroke}\textbf{State-of-the-art staging on stroke sleep.} On iSLEEPS MM-Net
reaches $\mmAcc$ accuracy, $\kappa$ $\mmK$, ahead of all eight deep baselines retrained on
identical folds in every fold, by $0.042$ to $0.164$ (Table~\ref{tab:bench}), and ahead of the
fine-tuned foundation encoder used on its own by $0.024$ ($p=0.014$).

\item \label{c:ahi}\textbf{Respiratory events and the AHI from the same model.} MM-Net detects
respiratory events at $\mmAuc$ AUC, $0.152$ above gradient boosting on the same
cardiorespiratory signals, and estimates each patient's AHI with $\rho=\mmRho$ against the
annotated index, closer than the clinical index itself agrees with the annotations
($\rho=0.687$; Section~\ref{sec:ahi}).

\item \label{c:cohorts}\textbf{Evidence across five cohorts and three settings.} Beyond
in-cohort cross-validation, MM-Net trained on ISRUC-I reaches $\isrAcc$ accuracy, reaches
$0.864$ on healthy sleep, and applied without retraining stages ISRUC-I at $0.730$ and
detects respiratory events on two external apnea cohorts at $0.83$--$0.84$ AUC with AHI $\rho$
of $0.63$--$0.65$ (Section~\ref{sec:external}). The same baselines are rerun on ISRUC-I with MM-Net's folds.

\item \label{c:physio}\textbf{Physiological attribution and deployment guidance.} An
interventional ablation shows each stream serves the output its physiology is closest to,
cortical channels for staging and respiratory effort for events, and the read-out does not
depend on the airflow channel the events are scored from. Across cohorts we identify nasal
pressure as a cohort-specific input and give a seven-channel variant for transfer
(Section~\ref{sec:attrib}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related}

\subsection{Deep Sleep Staging}
DeepSleepNet paired convolutional feature extraction with a bidirectional
LSTM~\cite{supratak2017deepsleepnet}; SeqSleepNet framed the night as sequence-to-sequence
labeling~\cite{phan2019seqsleepnet}; AttnSleep added attention over multi-resolution
convolutions~\cite{eldele2021attnsleep}; TinySleepNet and U-Time reduced the parameter budget
while holding accuracy~\cite{supratak2020tinysleepnet,perslev2019utime}; XSleepNet fused raw
and spectral views~\cite{phan2021xsleepnet}; SleepTransformer added interpretability and
uncertainty~\cite{sleeptransformer2022}; L-SeqSleepNet models whole-night
cycles~\cite{lseqsleepnet2023}; and recent backbones explore flexible channel sets and
state-space models~\cite{jirakittayakorn2024zleep,guo2024flexsleep,gu2023mamba,bitmamsleep2024}.
U-Sleep stages from arbitrary EEG and EOG derivations across many
cohorts~\cite{perslev2021usleep}. These models set accuracy on healthy corpora but depend on the
cortical micro-events that stroke disturbs, and they transfer poorly to stroke
patients~\cite{bose2026gap}.

\subsection{EEG Foundation Models}
LaBraM, CBraMod, EEGPT and BIOT learn transferable EEG representations from large unlabeled
corpora~\cite{labram2024,cbramod2025,eegpt2024,biot2023}; SleepFM extends the idea to several
PSG modalities~\cite{sleepfm2026}, and cross-scale models and benchmarks continue to
appear~\cite{csbrain2025,neuroatlas2026,sleepyland2026,estevan2025ssl}. These encoders are
usually either frozen or fine-tuned end to end on a single task. How to place a
\emph{fine-tuned} encoder inside a second, downstream model trained on the same patients has
received little attention, although it is the standard route to combining a foundation
representation with task-specific signals. The problem is the one stacked generalization
addresses~\cite{wolpert1992stacked}: a second-stage model must be trained on first-stage outputs
that are out of sample, which cross-fitting provides~\cite{chernozhukov2018dml}. We add the
step this setting needs and stacking does not, aligning the embedding spaces of the
cross-fitted encoders to the deployed encoder.

\subsection{Sleep-Disordered Breathing and Joint Models}
Apnea and hypopnea detectors read airflow and effort shape, oximetry and ECG-derived heart-rate
variability, usually at fine time resolution and for that task alone~\cite{aasm2023manual}.
Huttunen~\textit{et al.} trained one network to score sleep stages and respiratory events
simultaneously on $877$ clinical recordings~\cite{huttunen2023simultaneous}, and multitask
staging-and-apnea models have followed in elderly populations~\cite{multitask2025elderly}. None
has been shown on stroke, where the neural signal is itself damaged, and none combines a
foundation encoder with a cardiorespiratory stream or reports a validated AHI estimate across
cohorts.

\subsection{Sleep After Stroke}
Sleep-disordered breathing is common in the subacute phase and linked to poorer
outcome~\cite{bassetti2006sleep,denis2024sleep,tellenbach2023brainstem}, and focal injury
changes spindles, slow waves and REM
atonia~\cite{gottselig2002stroke,fernandez2019spindles,simpson2023laterality,facchin2020slowwaves,zheng2025spindles,anastasi2017bsi}.
Automated staging on ischemic stroke has so far been reported only as single-task baselines on
iSLEEPS~\cite{maiti2026isleeps,bose2026gap}. MM-Net addresses staging, respiratory events and the
AHI jointly on this population and validates each output beyond it.

\paragraph{iSLEEPS} The corpus holds overnight PSG from $100$ subacute ischemic-stroke patients
(mean age $50.5$; $77$ male), scored into the five AASM stages~\cite{maiti2026isleeps}. The
records of SN28 are byte-identical to those of SN15, so SN28 is excluded, giving $N=99$ and
$92{,}560$ epochs (Table~\ref{tab:dist}). The neural stream reads four EEG derivations (C4:M1,
C3:M2, O2:M1, O1:M2), two EOG channels (E1:M2, E2:M2) and the submental EMG at $100$\,Hz. The
cardiorespiratory stream reads ECG, oronasal thermal airflow, nasal pressure, thoracic,
abdominal and summed effort, SpO$_2$ and pulse, resampled by polyphase filtering to
$25$\,Hz. Snore is excluded because respiratory events are scored partly from the same airway
signal; limb, position and housekeeping traces carry no staging or breathing information.

\paragraph{Epoch alignment} iSLEEPS retains only epochs scored as a sleep stage, so a recording
whose hypnogram contains unscored rows has fewer retained epochs than $30$-second slots on the
clock. We cut every cardiorespiratory signal and every respiratory-event label at the true
recording time of each retained epoch. Cutting them on the clock grid instead silently shifts
them against the EEG after the first dropped row; in iSLEEPS this affects $63$ of the $100$
released recordings, by up to $137$ epochs. Respiratory events are mapped to epochs by onset, and the
annotated AHI is the number of apneas and hypopneas starting in scored sleep per hour of scored
sleep.

\paragraph{External cohorts} ISRUC-Sleep~\cite{khalighi2016isruc} uses the same central and
occipital derivations against the contralateral mastoid and records SpO$_2$, airflow, nasal
pressure, thoracic and abdominal effort and ECG; summed effort is formed as thorax plus abdomen,
and pulse as beat-to-beat heart rate from ECG R-peaks. Subgroup I comprises $100$ patients with
sleep disorders, II eight patients recorded on two nights, and III ten healthy adults; annotations
of the first rater are used. In $30$ of the $126$ ISRUC recordings the EDF header declares the
EEG range as $\pm25\,\mu$V, which places N3 at $9$--$23\,\mu$V peak to peak against $98$--$283\,\mu$V
in the other recordings and below the $75\,\mu$V that defines N3; we restore these channels
with a factor of ten, a correction decided from the header alone. The UCD database~\cite{ucddb2007}
holds $25$ adults referred for suspected sleep-disordered breathing, with AHI from $1.2$ to
$81.2$; it records only C3 and C4, which also feed the occipital inputs, and its headers carry no
physical unit, so EEG, EOG and EMG are scaled per recording to the iSLEEPS median whole-night
amplitude, with no label used. Sleep-EDF Cassette~\cite{kemp2000sleepedf} provides $40$ healthy
nights from $21$ subjects, with Fpz-Cz and Pz-Oz mapped to the central and occipital inputs.

\section{MM-Net}
\label{sec:method}

\subsection{Formulation}
A night is a sequence of $T$ epochs. For epoch $t$ MM-Net receives a neural vector
$\mathbf{f}_t=[\mathbf{d}_t;\mathbf{e}_t]\in\mathbb{R}^{388}$, the concatenation of $188$
engineered neural descriptors $\mathbf{d}_t$ and a $200$-dimensional foundation embedding
$\mathbf{e}_t$, and a raw cardiorespiratory tensor $\mathbf{x}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{8\times750}$, eight
channels at $25$\,Hz. It learns a subject-independent map
\begin{equation}
f_\theta:\big(\mathbf{f}_{1:T},\mathbf{x}_{1:T}\big)\ \longmapsto\
\big(\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{stg}}_{1:T},\ \hat a_{1:T}\big),
\end{equation}
where $\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{stg}}_t$ is a distribution over the five stages and
$\hat a_t\in[0,1]$ scores the onset of a respiratory event in epoch $t$. The AHI estimate is
derived from $\hat a_{1:T}$ and the decoded stages (Section~\ref{sec:ahimethod}).
Fig.~\ref{fig:arch} shows the architecture.

\begin{figure*}[t]

\subsection{Neural Descriptors}
\label{sec:features}
Per channel we take absolute and relative Welch band power in delta ($0.5$--$4$), theta
($4$--$8$), alpha ($8$--$13$), sigma ($11$--$16$) and beta ($16$--$30$)\,Hz, spectral entropy,
the $95\%$ spectral-edge and mean frequency, time-domain statistics and the three Hjorth
descriptors~\cite{hjorth1970eeg}; sigma-envelope spindle descriptors and slow-wave amplitude on
the EEG, ocular-movement energy on the EOG and tonic level on the EMG complete the $188$
(Appendix~\ref{app:features}). Descriptors are standardized per recording.

\subsection{Fine-Tuned Foundation Encoder}
\label{sec:encoder}
\paragraph{Encoder and input} LaBraM-Base~\cite{labram2024} ($5.8$\,M parameters) is
pretrained at $200$\,Hz on $3000$-sample windows. Each $30$-second epoch is resampled to
$200$\,Hz, split into two $15$-second windows, encoded and mean-pooled to
$\mathbf{e}_t\in\mathbb{R}^{200}$; amplitudes are scaled to $\mu$V$/100$. The four EEG derivations
enter as C4, C3, O2 and O1, and the two EOG derivations as F7 and F8, the frontal positions
nearest the eyes, so the encoder sees the ocular activity that separates REM from Wake.

\paragraph{Fine-tuning} Within each training fold the encoder is fine-tuned with a linear
staging head on single epochs of the training patients, with AdamW at $3\times10^{-5}$ for the
encoder and $10^{-3}$ for the head, a one-cycle schedule over three passes, batch size $64$ and
mixed precision, keeping the state with the best accuracy on the fold's validation patients.
Test patients never reach the encoder.

\paragraph{Cross-fitting} An encoder fine-tuned on a patient embeds that patient in-sample.
If the sequence model is trained on such embeddings and tested on out-of-sample ones, the two
distributions differ and the sequence model learns to trust a representation that is cleaner
than it will see at test time. We therefore split each fold's training patients into $K=3$
parts $P_1,P_2,P_3$ and fine-tune encoder $\phi_k$ on the training patients outside $P_k$;
every training patient $i\in P_k$ is embedded by $\phi_k$, an encoder that never saw it. The
full-fold encoder $\phi$, fine-tuned on all training patients, embeds the validation and test
patients.

\paragraph{Alignment} Cross-fitting makes every embedding out of sample but places the
training patients in the spaces of $K$ different encoders, which do not coincide with the
space of $\phi$ used at test time: fine-tuning runs from the same initialization, but the
resulting coordinates drift. We map each $\phi_k$ into the space of $\phi$ with a ridge
regression~\cite{hoerl1970ridge} fitted on the patients of $P_k$, for whom both embeddings exist,
\begin{equation}
W_k=\arg\min_W\ \big\lVert(E^{\phi}_{P_k}-\mu^{\phi}_k)-(E^{\phi_k}_{P_k}-\mu^{\phi_k}_k)W\big\rVert_F^2
+\lambda n_k\lVert W\rVert_F^2,
\end{equation}
and use $\tilde{\mathbf{e}}_t=(\mathbf{e}^{\phi_k}_t-\mu^{\phi_k}_k)W_k+\mu^{\phi}_k$ for the
training patients, with $\lambda=0.01$ and $n_k$ the number of epochs in $P_k$. The map uses
only training patients. Every training embedding is thereby out of sample and expressed in the
coordinates the sequence model sees at test time. Table~\ref{tab:components} shows that both
properties are required.

\subsection{Cardiorespiratory Encoder}
A three-layer convolutional encoder reads the raw $8\times750$ tensor with kernels of $25$
samples (one second) and channel widths $48$, $96$ and $96$, each followed by batch
normalization and GELU, with max-pooling by four after the first two layers; mean and max
pooling over time are concatenated and projected to $c_t\in\mathbb{R}^{64}$. Channels are
standardized per recording.

\subsection{Fusion, Temporal Decoder and Heads}
The neural vector is projected by a two-layer perceptron with LayerNorm to $n_t\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$.
The two embeddings are treated as tokens with learned modality embeddings and fused by four-head
cross-attention, so each modality reads the other, then projected to $z_t\in\mathbb{R}^{128}$.
A two-layer bidirectional LSTM with $256$ units per direction reads $z_t$ over a window of
$L=20$ epochs ($10$ minutes), giving $h_t\in\mathbb{R}^{512}$. The staging head is
$\hat{\mathbf{y}}^{\mathrm{stg}}_t=\mathrm{softmax}(W_s h_t)$. The respiratory head reads the
temporal state together with a direct copy of the cardiorespiratory embedding,
\begin{equation}
\hat a_t=\sigma\big(W_2\,\mathrm{GELU}(W_1[h_t;c_t])\big),
\end{equation}
so that the mechanical signature of an obstructed breath reaches the respiratory decision
without passing through a fusion trained mostly for staging. MM-Net has $2.85$\,M trainable
parameters besides the encoder.

\subsection{Training and Decoding}
Both tasks are trained jointly,
$\mathcal{L}=\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{stg}}+\mathcal{L}^{\mathrm{apn}}$, with an unweighted
staging cross-entropy and a respiratory cross-entropy whose positive term is weighted by
$n_-/n_+$. Optimization uses AdamW ($3\times10^{-4}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$) with cosine
annealing, batch size $32$, gradient clipping at $5$ and early stopping on the validation
patients. Staging posteriors are decoded by a Viterbi pass whose transition matrix and prior are
counted from the training labels~\cite{chen2024hmm}. Three seeds ($42$, $1$, $7$) are trained per
fold; we report the single-model mean and the three-seed average of the softmax outputs.
Table~\ref{tab:hyper} collects the settings.

\subsection{AHI Estimation}
\label{sec:ahimethod}
Because $\hat a_t$ scores event onsets, its sum over the epochs decoded as sleep estimates the
number of events, and dividing by the decoded sleep time gives a rate,
\begin{equation}
\widehat{\mathrm{AHI}}=k\cdot\frac{\sum_{t:\hat s_t\neq\mathrm{W}}\hat a_t}{\tfrac{30}{3600}\,\lvert\{t:\hat s_t\neq\mathrm{W}\}\rvert}.
\end{equation}
The positive weighting of the respiratory loss inflates $\hat a_t$, so a single scale $k$ is
fitted in each fold as the median ratio of annotated to raw estimate over the fold's validation
patients and applied unchanged to its test patients. For transfer, $k$ is the median of the ten
iSLEEPS fold values ($0.459$), and nothing is refitted on the external cohort. Severity follows
the AASM bands ($<5$, $5$--$15$, $15$--$30$, $\geq30$ events/h).

\section{Experimental Protocol}
\label{sec:protocol}
On iSLEEPS all results use the same ten patient-independent folds. Within each fold a disjoint
set of training patients is held out for validation; validation patients drive every choice
(early stopping, encoder state, configuration, AHI scale), and test patients are only scored.
ISRUC-I uses ten subject-independent folds of $80$ training, $10$ validation and $10$ test
subjects; ISRUC-II and III together use six folds that keep both nights of a subject on the same
side; Sleep-EDF uses ten subject-independent folds. In transfer, one model per seed is trained on
all $99$ iSLEEPS patients ($11$ for validation) and applied to the external cohort with no
re-fitted parameter, threshold or transition matrix.

Staging is scored by accuracy, macro-F1 and Cohen's $\kappa$, respiratory detection by the
area under the ROC curve (AUC) and average precision (AP), and the AHI by Spearman's $\rho$, the
mean absolute error (MAE), severity-band accuracy and linearly weighted $\kappa$. Values are
means over the ten test folds with their standard deviation. Paired comparisons use the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test on the ten fold means with Holm correction~\cite{holm1979simple}
across baselines; with ten folds the smallest attainable two-sided $p$ is $0.002$, and $0.016$
after Holm correction over eight comparisons.

\paragraph{Baselines} Eight deep architectures are retrained on the iSLEEPS folds: AttnSleep,
the corpus LSTM, 1D-ResNet-SE-LSTM, AT-BiLSTM, SleepTransformer, TinySleepNet, U-Time and
Huttunen~\textit{et al.}, the closest joint staging-and-respiratory system, ported from its
authors' code. Each reimplementation reproduces its published Sleep-EDF accuracy
(Table~\ref{tab:reimpl}). For respiratory detection, a desaturation rule, logistic regression and
gradient boosting read the $14$ engineered cardiorespiratory descriptors on the same folds.

\subsection{Where the Gain Comes From}
Table~\ref{tab:components} builds MM-Net from its frozen-encoder starting point one change at a
time, every row on the same folds and seeds. Uniform class weights and cross-attention fusion
contribute $+0.009$ together. The decisive step is the encoder. A fine-tuned encoder whose
training embeddings are cross-fitted but not aligned moves the three-seed accuracy by only
$+0.002$ ($p=0.63$) and is below the frozen encoder in five folds of ten, by up to $0.023$ in
the fold where the cross-fitted and full encoders agree least (embedding similarity $0.53$).
Aligning the spaces
converts the same encoder into $+0.014$ ($0.755\rightarrow0.769$, $p=0.020$), and in the single
model $+0.015$ in nine folds of ten ($p=0.027$). Adding the ocular channels to the encoder raises
the encoder's own accuracy from $0.709$ to $0.732$ and gave the joint-highest validation
accuracy ($0.779$), so it was carried forward; we report the variant validation selected,
not the one with the highest test score. Nasal pressure and the epoch alignment of
Section~\ref{sec:data} leave staging at $0.763$ and serve the respiratory head.

MM-Net is not the encoder alone. Used on its own, the fine-tuned encoder stages single epochs at
$0.732$; inside MM-Net, with the engineered descriptors, the cardiorespiratory stream and the
temporal decoder, the same encoder yields $0.756$ as a single model, $+0.024$ in eight folds of
ten ($p=0.014$). Fig.~\ref{fig:tsne} shows what fine-tuning changes in the representation: held-out
epochs that the frozen encoder spreads across one mixed cloud form stage-specific clusters after
fine-tuning, with REM, N3 and Wake separated (silhouette by stage $-0.045\rightarrow0.047$).

\subsection{Physiological Attribution}
\label{sec:attrib}

a time and retraining (Table~\ref{tab:ablate}; this analysis was run on the frozen-encoder
configuration, whose two streams are the same). Removing the cardiorespiratory stream lowers
respiratory AUC by $0.128$ (Holm $p=0.018$) and leaves staging untouched; removing the EEG lowers
staging by $0.059$ (Holm $p=0.018$) and leaves respiratory detection untouched. Within the

($-0.038$ AUC), the physiological signature of an obstructive event while it happens. Removing
the airflow channel the events were scored from leaves detection at $0.778$, so the read-out is not
a reproduction of the scoring signal. Training both tasks jointly is better than training either
alone, by $0.006$ staging accuracy ($p=0.014$) and $0.007$ respiratory AUC ($p=0.049$), with the
same architecture and only the loss changed.

$\rho=0.631$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:ahi}). On UCD, an apnea cohort recorded on a different system with
only central EEG, respiratory detection reaches $0.841$ AUC and AHI $\rho=0.653$, with $60\%$ of
patients in the correct severity band (weighted $\kappa$ $0.51$); staging there ($0.607$) reflects
the missing occipital and unit-less EEG, not the respiratory stream. Respiratory detection
transfers essentially intact: $0.83$--$0.84$ AUC on the two apnea cohorts against $0.825$
in-cohort.

Two deployment observations follow. First, the fine-tuned encoder's staging advantage is
realized when MM-Net is trained on the target cohort (Table~\ref{tab:isruccv}); applied
unchanged, the fine-tuned and frozen encoders stage alike ($0.730$ against $0.725$ on ISRUC-I).
Second, nasal pressure is cohort-specific: the eight-channel model, which reads it, transfers
with $0.794$ respiratory AUC and AHI $\rho$ $0.449$ on ISRUC-I, while the seven-channel variant
without it reaches $0.833$ and $0.631$. MM-Net therefore uses all eight channels when trained on a
cohort and the seven-channel variant when applied to a new acquisition system.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
MM-Net turns the polysomnogram of a stroke patient into the three outputs of a sleep report,
hypnogram, respiratory events and AHI, from a single model, and it does so more accurately than
any staging architecture we could bring to this cohort. Three findings carry beyond the
model.

\paragraph{Foundation encoders need out-of-sample, aligned embeddings downstream} The most
transferable result is methodological. A fine-tuned foundation encoder is a better stager by
itself, yet plugging it into a downstream model gave no significant gain until its training
embeddings were cross-fitted and aligned. The failure mode, a downstream model trained on
in-sample or misaligned representations, applies to any pipeline that fine-tunes a foundation
model and then trains a second model on its outputs, and the remedy costs only $K$ additional
fine-tuning runs and a ridge regression per fold. It held on two independent cohorts:
$+0.014$ on iSLEEPS with everything else fixed, and $+0.018$ for the complete model over the
frozen-encoder configuration on ISRUC-I.

\paragraph{Stroke sleep is not out of reach} Earlier work on iSLEEPS found every architecture in
a narrow band below $0.75$ and read it as a property of the injured brain~\cite{bose2026gap}.
MM-Net moves past that band on the same patients and folds, $0.763$, and reaches $0.803$ on a
sleep-disorder cohort and $0.864$ on healthy sleep with one unchanged architecture. The gap to
healthy sleep is narrower than previously reported, and the components that close it,
foundation representations adapted to sleep and the full multimodal recording, are
identified.

\paragraph{The AHI can come from the staging model} A dedicated detector is not needed to obtain
a usable AHI. MM-Net's respiratory head, read over its own decoded sleep, estimates the AHI with
$\rho=0.765$ in-cohort and $0.63$--$0.65$ on external cohorts, and its severity bands agree
with the annotations at weighted $\kappa$ up to $0.51$. For stroke units without polysomnography
technicians, a single automated pass delivering stages, events and severity is the practical
form of screening.

\paragraph{Scope} The evidence comes from offline whole-night analysis: staging is decoded after
the recording ends, which suits overnight polysomnography and not real-time monitoring.
Stroke-specific claims rest on iSLEEPS, the only public stroke polysomnography cohort; the four
external cohorts establish generalization across populations and acquisition systems. N1 remains
the hardest stage on every cohort and for every method, and the AHI severity band is the output
with most room to grow; event-level training on larger annotated cohorts is the natural next step
for both.

%=====================================================================
\section{Conclusion}
MM-Net reads the full polysomnogram and delivers sleep stages, respiratory events and the AHI
in one pass. Its fine-tuned EEG foundation encoder, integrated through cross-fitting and ridge
alignment, lifts staging on ischemic-stroke sleep to $0.763$ accuracy, ahead of every baseline in
every fold, and to $0.803$ on ISRUC-I and $0.864$ on healthy sleep. Respiratory events are detected
at $0.825$ AUC and the AHI is estimated with $\rho=0.765$, and both transfer to external
cohorts without retraining. Code, features and trained models are released.

\section{The $188$ Neural Descriptors}
\label{app:features}
Each $30$-second epoch is $3000$ samples at $100$\,Hz, and every descriptor below is
computed on one epoch alone; the cross-epoch context is supplied by the recurrent decoder.
Twenty-three descriptors are computed on each of the seven neural channels, C4:M1, C3:M2,
O2:M1, O1:M2, E1:M2, E2:M2 and the chin EMG, which gives $161$; a further $27$ event
descriptors are applied only to the channels whose physiology defines them, giving $188$.

The per-channel set begins with the spectrum, a Welch estimate over $256$-sample
segments overlapping by $128$. From it we take absolute and relative power in the
five bands of Section~\ref{sec:features}, the logarithm of total power, spectral
entropy on the normalised spectrum, the $95\%$ spectral-edge frequency and the mean
frequency. Six time-domain statistics follow, namely standard deviation,
peak-to-peak, root mean square, skewness, kurtosis and zero-crossing rate, and to
these are added the three Hjorth descriptors, activity, mobility and complexity,
computed from the first and second differences~\cite{hjorth1970eeg}.

The event descriptors follow the graphoelements a scorer reads, and every band-pass
among them is a fourth-order Butterworth applied forwards and backwards so that
phase is preserved. Spindles contribute three descriptors per EEG channel: the
channel is filtered to $11$--$16$\,Hz, its analytic envelope is taken by Hilbert
transform, and the threshold is set at the $90$th percentile of that envelope within
the epoch; density counts upward crossings of that threshold, and the mean and
standard deviation of the envelope complete the three. Slow waves contribute two per
EEG channel, the peak-to-peak and the mean absolute amplitude of the $0.5$--$4$\,Hz
band. Each EOG channel contributes the log mean squared first difference and the
$95$th percentile of its absolute value, which separate the fast deflections of REM
from slow drift, and the chin EMG contributes log root-mean-square, the $90$th
percentile of the absolute signal, and the log variance of the first difference,
which together track tonic level and its loss in REM. Non-finite values, which arise
only from a flat channel, are set to zero, and each descriptor is standardised against
the mean and standard deviation of its own recording.
